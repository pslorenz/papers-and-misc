# Backup Is the Easy Half

Unreleased
Steven Lorenz

*The backups exist. They ran last night. None of that is the question. The question is whether the business can come back from the incident the backups were meant to protect against, and most small businesses have never tested.*

---

The attacker has been inside the law firm's tenant for eleven days before anything interesting happens, and the firm will not know for three more weeks. How the attacker got in is the subject of the previous piece in this series, and it is not the part that matters now. What matters is what they do once they are in.

On day one, they read email. Three mailboxes worth, to start, which gives them a working map of the firm in about an hour. Who signs off on wires. Who handles client intake. Who the managing partner's assistant is, and what her schedule looks like. Which clients are active and which ones have quiet matters that nobody talks about over unencrypted channels. The mailbox they took belongs to a paralegal with Helpdesk Administrator rights she does not remember being granted, assigned during an onboarding push in 2022 by an IT contractor who is no longer with the firm. The paralegal has never used the rights. The attacker will.

On day three, they find the backup appliance. Its web administration interface is accessible from inside the office, and the managing partner's bookmarks bar includes a shortcut labeled "BACKUP ADMIN" pointing at it. The credentials are saved in his browser. His browser session is one of the sessions the attacker now controls. They log in. They read the configuration. They take notes. They log out.

On day seven, they change the cloud backup retention policy from ninety days down to seven. They disable the email alert that would have fired on that change, because one of the accounts they control now has the rights to do that. They note the schedule of the off-site replication. They watch the weekly invoice run on Thursday afternoon and confirm how the firm's banking platform authenticates, which is through the same Microsoft identity they already own. They wait.

On day twenty-three, a Friday, at 4:47 in the afternoon, the attacker reaches the backup appliance through the managing partner's still-valid session, factory resets it, and then detonates ransomware across the on-premises file servers. On Sunday evening, when the firm realizes what has happened, they discover three things in sequence. The appliance in the closet is a doorstop. The cloud replica still exists, but the retention change made sixteen days ago has aged every pre-attack backup point out of the system, so what remains is a complete, protected, beautifully stored copy of seven-day-old encrypted files. Even if they had clean data, restoring 800 gigabytes over their office internet connection was never going to finish before Thursday.

This is not a story about incompetence. The firm was doing what every competent IT person would have told them to do in 2018. Backup appliance on-premises, replicate to cloud, ninety-day retention. Industry-standard practice. The industry-standard practice was the problem.

I want to be careful about what this piece is about. It is not about backup. The backups existed. They ran. They replicated. On Friday at 4:46 every status light was green, and on Monday at 9:00 the firm could not open a case file. The gap between those two moments is the entire subject. That gap is where most small businesses die, quietly, without making the news, because their attention was on the thing that was already solved rather than the thing that was not.

The previous piece in this series argued that identity has become the operational spine of the business and that the security question has changed from "how strong is the login" to "what does an attacker get when the login fails." This piece takes the next step. Once the attacker gets in, the question is whether the business can come back. Most of what is sold as the answer to that question is something else in a similar box, and the difference does not show up until the day it matters.

## Backup is the easy half. Recovery is the hard half.

I want to coin a distinction and use it for the rest of the piece.

Backup is copying your data to another location. That is the easy half. It is a solved problem, technically and commercially. Every productivity suite does some version of it by default. Every managed service provider sells a layer of it on top. The appliance in the closet, the replication to cloud, the retention policy, the nightly job, the status email that comes through every morning saying the backup succeeded. All of that is backup. It is not the hard part, and the fact that it is the part the industry spends most of its time on is itself a clue that something is wrong.

Recovery is getting the business back to operational condition within a timeframe the business can survive. That is the hard half. It is a systems problem, a logistics problem, a decision-making problem, and a bandwidth problem before it is ever a data problem. The data is the ingredient. Recovery is the meal. Most small businesses have bought ingredients and assume they have bought dinner.

The reframe I want you to carry out of this piece is that you do not have a backup problem, you have a recovery capability problem, and recovery capability is the thing you actually need to build, test, and maintain. Backup is a component of recovery capability. It is not a substitute for it. When the question "are we backed up" becomes "can we recover," most of the confidence in the answer evaporates, and the evaporation is the important signal. If you can answer the first question yes and the second question with only a guess, you are in the same room as the law firm. The lights are still green. Friday has not happened yet.

The rest of the piece is about closing the gap between the two questions.

## How conventional backups fail under attack

The conventional small-business backup architecture was designed for a threat model that no longer describes the actual risk. The threat model it was designed for was hardware failure, building fire, flood, and stolen laptops. Against those threats, the architecture works. Against the category of attack actually running against small businesses in 2026, which starts with identity compromise and ends with coordinated destruction of both production and backup, the architecture has holes you can drive a truck through.

I want to show you the holes specifically, because generic advice about ransomware is the kind of thing that becomes noise. The table below maps common threat scenarios against what a conventional backup configuration will actually do. Conventional here means what I described at the firm: backup appliance on-premises, replicating to a cloud service, with retention policies and alerting, all administered through accounts in the same identity provider the business uses for everything else.

| Threat | Conventional backup survives? | Why or why not |
|---|---|---|
| Drive fails on a single server | Yes | This is the threat the architecture was designed for. Restore from last night's backup, lose at most a day. |
| User deletes a file by accident | Yes | Retention covers this cleanly. Self-service restore in most platforms. |
| Laptop lost or stolen | Yes | The laptop's data either syncs to a cloud store that is backed up, or is a copy of data that lives elsewhere. |
| Insider deletes production data | Usually | Depends entirely on whether the insider has administrative access to the backup system. If they do not, the backup survives and recovery is straightforward. If they do, the insider is the same threat as ransomware below. |
| Ransomware on endpoints only | Usually | Production file servers are untouched, backups untouched. Rebuild the endpoint, restore the user's files, move on. |
| Ransomware reaching the file servers | Partial at best | If the backup appliance is on the same domain and reachable from the same network, it is encrypted alongside everything else. The cloud replica may be fine, but see the next row. |
| Full identity compromise with coordinated backup destruction | No | Attacker with administrative rights in the tenant can reach the backup management interface, change retention, disable alerts, and then destroy production. The cloud replica exists but contains only post-compromise data by the time anyone notices. This is the attack pattern in the opening of this piece. |
| Catastrophic building loss, fire, or flood | Yes, if cloud replication is current | The architecture is genuinely good at this. The off-site copy is the point. |
| Extended internet outage at the office | Recovery stalls | Data is safe but restoring it back across the office connection is measured in days, not hours. Discussed in the bandwidth section below. |

A few things should jump off the table.

The architecture is genuinely effective against the original threat model, and it would be wrong to say it is useless. If your concern is disk failure and accidental deletion, what you have is what you need. The concern I want you to take seriously is that the dominant attack pattern in 2026 is not in that original threat model. It is in the last two or three rows of the table, and against those rows, the conventional architecture gives you a false sense of a defense you do not actually have.

Let me walk through the specific mechanisms.

The first failure mode is that the backup appliance is joined to the domain. This is the default configuration for almost every on-premises backup product, because it makes authentication and management easier. The tradeoff, unstated at purchase, is that an attacker who compromises the domain gets the backup appliance in the same motion. Ransomware operators know this. Every mature ransomware playbook includes the step of enumerating and neutralizing backup systems before detonating, because a recovered victim does not pay. The domain-joined appliance is the easiest possible target for that step.

The second failure mode is shared credentials. The account that administers the backup system is often the same account, or a closely related account, that administers the tenant. Sometimes it is the managing partner's own Global Administrator account, reused because setting up a separate identity felt like unnecessary friction. When the tenant is compromised, the backup system is compromised in the same breath, without any additional effort on the attacker's part.

The third failure mode is the word "immutable." This word appears in the marketing material for almost every modern backup product, and it does not mean what the reader thinks it means. In the weakest sense, it means that the data cannot be deleted without going through an administrative interface that logs the action. In a stronger sense, it means the data cannot be deleted by a user, but can be deleted by an administrator of the backup system. In the strongest sense, which is the sense that matters, it means the data cannot be deleted or altered by anyone, for a defined retention period, regardless of credentials. The first two senses do not survive an attacker who has administrative control of the tenant. Only the third does. When a product is described as "immutable" without a qualifier, the default assumption should be the weak sense until you have verified otherwise.

The fourth failure mode is retention windows shorter than attacker dwell time. The industry average time that an attacker is inside a small-business tenant before detection is somewhere between two and four weeks, depending on which report you read. If your backup retention is fourteen days, every backup point predating the compromise ages out of the system during the compromise. By the time anyone notices, the clean copies are gone. If your retention is ninety days but an attacker quietly changed it to seven three weeks before detonating, you have the same problem by a different path, which is what happened to the law firm.

The fifth failure mode is bandwidth. The cloud replica is a recovery insurance policy whose claim is paid in bytes over the office internet connection. Eight hundred gigabytes over a 200-megabit upload link is between fifteen and twenty hours under perfect conditions and several days under the realistic ones, and that is assuming the cloud service can deliver at line rate, which it often cannot. If the attack takes down more than a few servers worth of data, the restore time is measured in business days, not hours, which is the gap between "we have backups" and "we are operational."

Each of these failure modes has shown up in real incidents at real small businesses in the last twenty-four months. They are not theoretical. They are the shape of the problem as it actually presents. The common thread across all of them is that the backup architecture was evaluated against the old threat model and found acceptable, and the new threat model was never applied to it. What to do about that is the rest of this piece.

## The rule you learned, and the two things to add to it

If you have been exposed to backup thinking at all, you have probably encountered the 3-2-1 rule. Three copies of your data. Two different storage media. One copy offsite. It is a reasonable rule, developed for the threat model of fire and hardware failure, and it is still the right floor. What it is not is a ceiling, although it has become one in most people's minds because nothing has replaced it in the small-business conversation.

The version of the rule that engages with current threats adds two items. Three copies, two media, one offsite, one immutable, one tested.

Immutable in the sense that matters means that the copy cannot be altered or destroyed by any administrator for a defined retention period. This is a property, not a marketing claim. The way to verify it is to try to break it. Log in with the most privileged account the backup system recognizes and attempt to delete a protected backup point. If you can, it is not immutable, and you should know that before the attacker does. If you cannot, and the system refuses even the most privileged credential, you have the property you need. There are commercial backup products that provide this. There are configurations of cloud object storage that provide this at a lower cost. The specific implementation matters less than verifying the property actually holds.

Tested in the sense that matters means that in the last twelve months, you have performed a full restore to an isolated environment, measured how long it took, documented what broke, and used the result to update your recovery plan. Not "we verified that the backup ran last night." Not "we restored a file to check." A full restore of a representative system or workload, end to end, with a stopwatch. If you have not done this in the last year, you do not know what your actual recovery time is. You have a documented recovery time. That is a different thing.

These two additions do not replace the classical rule. They extend it. Three copies, two media, one offsite handles the original threat model of hardware and disaster. One immutable handles the adversarial threat model of coordinated attack on the backup system. One tested handles the operational threat model of the plan that looked fine on paper and turns out not to work when it matters. All three threat models are real. You need to have answered all three.

A rule is only as useful as the routine that keeps it true over time, so I want to say explicitly: these are not one-time checks. The immutable property can be broken by a configuration change, an upgrade, or a policy update. The tested property decays every month that passes without a new test. Both are conditions to maintain, not boxes to check. In practice that means revisiting both at least yearly, as scheduled work, not as a response to a scare.

## The restore you have never performed

Here is an uncomfortable question. If your entire production environment disappeared tonight, and you had to rebuild it from backup, how long would it take? Not the number in the runbook. The number that would actually be measured.

If you have a confident answer to that question, you are in a small minority. Most small businesses have a documented recovery time objective that was chosen during procurement, written into a contract, and never validated against reality. The number is aspirational. The actual number is unknown, and the gap between aspirational and actual is where the business falls over during an incident.

I want to make the case that the single most valuable thing you can do in the next quarter, more valuable than buying better tooling or upgrading your backup contract, is to perform an honest restore test. The first test will be informative in ways you do not expect. It will also probably fail in ways that you will only be able to fix because you have discovered them. That is the point. You are trading a small failure now, in a controlled environment, for a much larger failure later in an uncontrolled one.

A realistic restore test at small-business scale is not the enterprise exercise you may be imagining. You do not need a full-scale disaster recovery simulation with an outside vendor. You need to restore something real to an isolated environment and measure what happens. A twenty-person company can do this with a spare laptop, a cloud virtual machine that costs a few dollars for the afternoon, or a corner of an existing test environment.

Pick one system that genuinely matters. A file server, a critical SaaS tenant's export, one user's full workstation. Restore it to an environment that is not connected to production. Measure the time from the start of the restore to the moment the system is functional in a way you could demonstrate to a colleague. Document what you had to do along the way. Document what was missing. Document what you had to look up. That documentation is the output of the exercise, not the restored system itself.

The first test almost always surfaces the same categories of problem, and I want to name them so that when they show up in yours, you will recognize them rather than panic.

You will discover dependencies you did not know existed. The application server cannot start without the license server, which cannot start without DNS, which is running on the domain controller, which you did not restore first because you forgot that the other systems depended on it. The order of operations matters and was never documented, because nobody has ever had to do this in the correct order before.

You will discover credentials you do not have. The service account password is in someone's password manager, and that person is on vacation, and the password manager itself depends on the identity provider that is part of what you are restoring. You will find that you have been trusting the continuous availability of the thing you are recovering to make the recovery possible, which is a circular dependency you could only have noticed by trying to break it.

You will discover that certificates have expired. Backups capture certificate files. Certificate files have expiration dates. The expiration date was set when the certificate was issued, not when it was backed up. A certificate backed up three months ago might be ninety days from expiring, or it might already have expired and the production system was quietly renewing it continuously. The restored system has no idea about any of this and will refuse to serve requests.

You will discover third-party integrations that depend on your original public IP address, or your original external hostname, or your original API keys that have long since been rotated in production and never updated in documentation. These will fail silently, because the third parties are receiving connections from a different address and will simply reject them without a useful error message.

None of this is a failure of the test. This is the test doing exactly what it is supposed to do, which is surface the work that has to happen before the first real restore rather than during it. The first test is never clean. The fifth test might be. You cannot skip the first four to get there.

The cadence I recommend for small businesses is a lightweight quarterly partial test, plus one annual full test of a realistic scenario. The quarterly test is small: restore one thing, measure one thing, update one piece of documentation. The annual test is the full scenario: a plausible ransomware event, restored to an isolated environment, with the realistic sequence of systems and dependencies. Both are achievable at small scale. Neither is happening in most tenants right now, which is why most tenants will find out their actual recovery time by accident.

## Economics at small scale

I want to engage honestly with money, because pretending that the enterprise architecture is achievable for a twenty-person firm is one of the reasons the industry's advice on this topic lands so badly. The textbook recommendation, which is backup appliance plus cloud replication plus immutable offsite plus annual third-party disaster recovery testing, is tens of thousands of dollars a year. That is not in most small-business budgets, and pretending otherwise leads either to a purchase the business cannot sustain or to nothing happening at all, which is the more common outcome.

I am going to describe three tiers of what recovery capability looks like at different budget levels. None of these are product recommendations. They are descriptions of what the architecture contains at each tier and what you are and are not buying. Your specific vendor choices are less important than whether the architecture at your tier has the right properties.

At the minimum viable tier, which for a small business runs in the low hundreds of dollars per month, the architecture is: cloud object storage with true immutability enabled, a backup tool that can write to it and read from it, and a disciplined quarterly manual restore test. The object storage has its own credentials, not shared with your primary identity provider, and those credentials are held by one or two people who do not routinely administer anything else. Retention is at least ninety days. This tier handles file-level data well and handles whole-system recovery poorly. If your business can survive by rebuilding systems from scratch and re-pointing them at restored file data, the tier is sufficient. If you need to recover working server images, this tier does not give you that, and you should know that going in.

At the middle tier, which for a small business runs roughly between five hundred and fifteen hundred dollars per month, the architecture adds image-level snapshots of servers and workstations, a purpose-built backup service with its own identity plane separate from your productivity suite, and the same immutable cloud storage underneath. You can rebuild systems, not just files, and you can do it faster because the images include operating system, applications, and configuration. This is the tier where the standard small-business IT practice has landed, and for most businesses, it is the right target. It is not the cheapest tier, but it is the cheapest tier that honestly addresses the threat model of coordinated attack. The money difference between this tier and the minimum viable tier is what you pay for the ability to recover without rebuilding from scratch.

At the full-featured tier, which for a small business runs between fifteen hundred and several thousand dollars per month, the architecture adds continuous replication with low recovery point objectives, standby infrastructure in the cloud that can be activated in hours rather than days, and an annual third-party tested exercise against a written playbook. For most small businesses, this tier is more than they need. It is the right tier for regulated businesses, businesses whose hourly downtime cost is high enough to justify the spend, and businesses where the client contracts specifically require it. If you are not in one of those categories, the money between the middle tier and this tier is probably better spent elsewhere.

The important point about the tier framing is that moving up tiers is not the main lever. The main lever is operating whichever tier you chose well. A well-tested minimum viable architecture with verified immutable storage and a proven quarterly restore routine will beat an untested full-featured architecture every single time the bill comes due. The money matters less than the discipline. The reason to discuss budget at all is not to push anyone up a tier, it is to dispel the idea that recovery capability requires enterprise spending. It does not. It requires appropriate spending matched by the work to actually use what you bought.

## The recovery decisions nobody plans for

Recovery is not a purely technical exercise. It is a sequence of business decisions made under pressure by tired people, and the quality of those decisions determines whether the technical recovery matters. I have watched businesses with excellent backups make poor recovery decisions and land worse than businesses with adequate backups and good decisions. The technical capability is the ingredient. The decisions are the meal.

There are a small number of decisions that every small business will face during a recovery event, and that are almost never documented in advance. I want to name them, because knowing they exist is more than half the work of addressing them.

The first decision is recovery order. When your environment is down and you can bring systems back one at a time, which goes first? The answer is not "the most important system." The answer is the system that, once running, unblocks the most other work. For most small businesses this is identity. After identity, it is the system the business actually uses to make money tomorrow morning, which varies by business. A law firm restores the case management system before the marketing website. An accounting practice restores the tax software before the internal wiki. The answer is specific to the business and needs to be written down before the incident, because at 2 AM on a Sunday nobody is in the state of mind to work it out from first principles.

The second decision is acceptable data loss. In a recovery event, you are restoring to some point in the past, and the gap between that point and the moment the attack happened is data the business will not get back. How much of that are you willing to accept? A day's worth of unbilled hours? A week of customer orders? This is a conversation to have with the owner or the leadership team before the incident, not during it. The recovery point objective written in the contract is not the answer. The answer is what the owner says when asked directly, and that answer should inform the retention and replication choices the rest of the architecture makes.

The third decision is about ransom. Most small businesses have a policy on paying a ransom that consists of the CEO shrugging if you ask them, and a different policy in practice once it is their business that is down. The policy should be decided cold, in writing, before the decision ever has to be made. Whether or not the business pays under specific conditions. Who has the authority to authorize payment. What the legal and insurance implications are, which your attorney and your cyber insurance carrier both have opinions about that you want to have heard in advance rather than in the middle. The ransom decision is the kind of decision that is painful to make calmly and nearly impossible to make well under duress. Make it calmly.

The fourth decision is communication. During a recovery event, the business is communicating with clients, with employees, with vendors, with insurance, and possibly with law enforcement and regulators. Each of those audiences needs different information at different times. Without a plan, the communication happens ad hoc, inconsistently, and often in ways that make the legal and insurance situations worse. A written communication plan does not need to be long. It needs to name who speaks to each audience, what the approval chain is for external statements, and what the secondary communication channel is if the primary one is down.

The fifth decision is the degraded operating mode. Most businesses, during a recovery event, have the option to operate in a reduced capacity rather than wait for full recovery. This is often the right choice. It is also a decision that has to be made explicitly, because operating degraded has its own risks (clients served with incomplete information, decisions made with incomplete data, errors that have to be reconciled later). The rules for when to operate degraded, and how degraded is too degraded, should be written down before the event, not invented during it.

None of these decisions require a large document. A two-page recovery decision playbook, stored offline, is better than anything most small businesses have now. The work is not the writing. The work is making the decisions in advance, which most people avoid because the decisions are uncomfortable. Making them in advance is the entire value.

## Where to start

The piece above is more work than anyone can absorb at once. Here is the triaged version, organized the same way as the previous piece in this series. The first list is work to do this week. The second list is work to scope and complete during the current quarter.

### This week

- Inventory your backup systems. Write down every place your data is copied, including the ones you may have forgotten about, such as workstation cloud sync, SaaS application exports, and departmental file shares with their own regimes. The list is often longer than expected, and some entries on it are not doing what the business thinks they are doing.
- For each backup system, verify the current retention setting by looking at it directly in the administrative console, not by trusting what was configured during onboarding. Retention policies drift. Attackers change them. Trial features expire. The number you see today is the number that matters, and it may not match what is in the contract.
- Identify which accounts have administrative access to each backup system, and which of those accounts share identity with your primary productivity suite. If the global administrator of your Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace tenant can also administer your backup system, those two concerns have collapsed into a single account, and the blast radius from the previous piece in this series is now the blast radius of your backups.
- Ask, directly, whether anyone has ever performed a full restore from your current backup system. Not a single file. A full system or a representative workload. If the answer is no, or if the answer is a cautious maybe, that is your answer, and it should drive the quarter plan.
- Check the last ninety days of the backup system's audit log for configuration changes to retention, destination, or alerting. If alerting is off or was recently changed, treat it as a signal to investigate further, not as a housekeeping finding.

### This quarter

- Add one immutable copy to your backup chain. The lowest-cost route is cloud object storage with object lock or equivalent retention enforcement, writing from your existing backup tool. The property you are buying is that the copy cannot be altered by any administrator, including you, for a defined period. Verify the property by trying to alter the data with the most privileged credential the system recognizes. If you succeed, you do not have what you paid for.
- Perform your first real restore test. Choose one system that matters, restore it to an isolated environment, measure end-to-end time, and document everything that went wrong along the way. Do not attempt to test everything. Test something. The goal of the first test is to generate a realistic restore time and a list of dependencies you had not previously documented.
- Write the two-page recovery decision playbook. Name the recovery order for your business, the acceptable data loss threshold, the ransom policy and authority, the communication plan, and the rules for degraded operation. Store the playbook somewhere that does not require the identity provider or the production environment to retrieve. Paper, a phone note that syncs offline, or a USB drive in the same safe as the break-glass credentials from the previous piece all work.
- Separate the backup administrative identity from the production administrative identity. The backup admin account should not be a member of your primary identity provider, or if it must be, it should be an isolated account that does not share any role with the accounts used for everything else. Multi-factor authentication on that account should be a hardware key, not a push notification.
- Review retention periods against current attacker dwell time assumptions. Ninety days is the minimum I would recommend for any small business in the current threat environment. If your retention is shorter than that, raise it before the quarter ends, and check whether the increase requires a storage quota adjustment that somebody will need to approve.

These items do not make you a sophisticated recovery operation. They make you an honest one, which is the more important thing. A recovery capability that is understood and practiced at the minimum viable tier is better than a recovery capability that exists in a slide deck at the full-featured tier. The work is to close the gap between what you think you have and what you actually have.

## What this leaves you with

The previous piece in this series was about the identity layer, which is the front door, and what an attacker gets when the front door fails. This piece is about what happens inside the house after the front door has failed, and whether the business can rebuild once the damage is known. These are two halves of the same conversation, which is resilience, and neither half is sufficient by itself. A business with strong identity controls and no tested recovery capability will eventually encounter an insider, a supply chain compromise, or a zero-day that routes around the identity controls, and will then discover that the recovery plan is untested. A business with tested recovery and weak identity controls will recover more often than it should have to, which is its own form of failure. Both halves are required.

I have been writing for two pieces now against a pattern I see repeatedly in small-business security spending, which is the tendency to buy more of what is already being done rather than to build the thing that is not. More endpoint protection, more email filtering, more user training, a better backup appliance. These purchases are easier to make than the work of testing a restore, writing a decision playbook, or revisiting administrative role assignments. The work is not free and it is not comfortable. It is the work that actually distinguishes the businesses that recover from the businesses that do not.

The challenge I want to leave you with is small and specific. In the next thirty days, test a restore. Choose something that matters. Restore it somewhere isolated. Measure what happens. Document what broke and what took longer than you expected. You do not need to test everything. You do not need to test the right thing. You need to test something, because the first test teaches you more than any article can. Whatever you learn from that test will change how you read every other piece of backup advice you encounter for the rest of your career, and that change is the point.

The law firm in the opening of this piece will recover, eventually, at a cost none of them will enjoy and some of them may not survive in the long run. There was a version of their Friday afternoon where the session was stolen and the ransomware fired and the backups came back, because the architecture had the properties that mattered and the team had done the work. That version was cheaper than the one they lived through, by a margin that makes the work look obvious in retrospect. The work always looks obvious in retrospect. The question is whether you are doing it now, while the retrospect is still hypothetical.
